> *"And will not God bring about justice for his chosen ones, who cry out to him day and night? ... However, when the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?"*
> — Luke 18:7–8

## The Widow Who Would Not Stop Knocking

Jesus told a story to make one point unmistakable: *always pray and never give up* (Luke 18:1).

In a certain town there was a judge who neither feared God nor cared what anyone thought — a hard man, immune to guilt and immune to pressure. And in that same town there was a widow, one of the most powerless people in the ancient world, with no husband, no standing, and no leverage. She had only one thing: persistence. She kept coming to the judge with the same plea — *"Grant me justice against my adversary"* (Luke 18:2–3).

For a while he refused. But she would not stop. And finally the judge said to himself, *"Even though I don't fear God or care what people think, yet because this widow keeps bothering me, I will see that she gets justice, so that she won't eventually come and attack me!"* (Luke 18:4–5). She wore him down. Not with power, but with persistence.

Then Jesus drives it home: *"Listen to what the unjust judge says. And will not God bring about justice for his chosen ones, who cry out to him day and night? Will he keep putting them off? I tell you, he will see that they get justice, and quickly"* (Luke 18:6–8).

Here is the logic. If even a corrupt, godless judge will eventually grant justice to a persistent widow, how much *more* will a good and loving God bring justice to His own children who cry out to Him day and night? The point is not that God is reluctant like the judge. The point is the opposite — He is nothing like the judge, so how much more certain is His answer.

But then Jesus ends with a question that hangs in the air over the whole sermon: *"When the Son of Man comes, will he find faith on the earth?"* The widow had it — the faith that keeps coming, keeps knocking, keeps crying out. The question is whether *we* will still have it when He returns. So the first thing we must ask is: what kind of faith is Jesus actually looking for?

## 1. Persistent Faith

The first mark of the faith Jesus looks for is that it *does not give up.* *"Ask and it will be given to you; seek and you will find; knock and the door will be opened to you"* (Matthew 7:7).

Notice these are not one-time actions. In the original language they carry the sense of *keep asking, keep seeking, keep knocking.* This is the widow's faith exactly. She did not ask once, get refused, and walk away. She came back, and back, and back. Many of us pray about something once or twice, hear nothing, and conclude the door is closed. But Jesus is describing a faith that keeps its hand on the door — asking, seeking, knocking — until heaven answers. That is the persistent faith He is looking for.

## 2. Faith with Actions

The second mark is that this faith *does something.* It is not merely believing the right facts; it is belief that moves.

James is blunt about it: *"Show me your faith without deeds, and I will show you my faith by my deeds. You believe that there is one God. Good! Even the demons believe that — and shudder"* (James 2:18–19). That last line should stop us. Simply *believing God exists* is not saving faith — the demons believe that much, and it does them no good. A faith that never acts is a faith even demons possess.

So what does living faith look like? James points to Abraham: *"Was not our father Abraham considered righteous for what he did when he offered his son Isaac on the altar? You see that his faith and his actions were working together, and his faith was made complete by what he did"* (James 2:20–22). Abraham did not just say he trusted God — he climbed the mountain, laid his son on the altar, and raised the knife. His faith and his obedience worked *together*, and the working was what completed the faith.

And so the verdict: *"Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness, and he was called God's friend. You see that a person is considered righteous by what they do and not by faith alone"* (James 2:23–24). The faith Jesus will look for on the last day is a persistent faith that also *acts* — belief and obedience, working together.

## 3. Heaven's Kingdom Court

Now the sermon lifts the curtain on something staggering: there is an actual courtroom in heaven. Daniel saw it in a vision. *"As I looked, thrones were set in place, and the Ancient of Days took his seat. His clothing was as white as snow; the hair of his head was white like wool. His throne was flaming with fire, and its wheels were all ablaze"* (Daniel 7:9).

This is not a metaphor Daniel invented. He *saw* it — the throne of God blazing with fire, the Ancient of Days seated to judge. And the scene grows: *"A river of fire was flowing, coming out from before him. Thousands upon thousands attended him; ten thousand times ten thousand stood before him. The court was seated, and the books were opened"* (Daniel 7:10).

Read those last words slowly. *The court was seated, and the books were opened.* Heaven keeps records. There is a court, there are books, and one day the court will be seated. This is the reality every human life is moving toward, whether we think about it or not.

## 4. The Court of Judgment

The Apostle John saw the same courtroom from another angle. *"Then I saw a great white throne and him who was seated on it... And I saw the dead, great and small, standing before the throne, and books were opened. Another book was opened, which is the book of life"* (Revelation 20:11–12).

Notice there are two kinds of books. There are books that record *what we have done* — and there is one more book, the *book of life*, that records *whose we are.* Both are opened. *"The dead were judged according to what they had done as recorded in the books... each person was judged according to what they had done"* (Revelation 20:12–13). Nothing is forgotten. Every deed, every word, every hidden thing is written down.

And the outcome is sobering: *"Then death and Hades were thrown into the lake of fire... Anyone whose name was not found written in the book of life was thrown into the lake of fire"* (Revelation 20:14–15). The deciding factor is not merely the record of deeds — it is whether your name is written in the book of life.

But hear the mercy in how Jesus describes His own role. *"If anyone hears my words but does not keep them, I do not judge that person. For I did not come to judge the world, but to save the world. There is a judge for the one who rejects me and does not accept my words; the very words I have spoken will condemn them at the last day"* (John 12:47–48). Jesus did not come the first time to condemn. He came to *save.* The condemnation at the last day falls only on those who reject the very words that were meant to rescue them.

## 5. The Court of Rewards — and Condemnation

The same courtroom that judges also *rewards.* *"Look, I am coming soon! My reward is with me, and I will give to each person according to what they have done. I am the Alpha and the Omega... Blessed are those who wash their robes, that they may have the right to the tree of life and may go through the gates into the city"* (Revelation 22:12–14).

For the faithful, the courtroom is not a place of terror but of reward. Jesus comes *with* His reward. Those who have washed their robes — cleansed by His blood — are given the right to the tree of life and welcomed through the gates of the city. That is the destiny of persistent, active faith.

But there is another side to the same court. *"Outside are the dogs, those who practice magic arts, the sexually immoral, the murderers, the idolaters and everyone who loves and practices falsehood"* (Revelation 22:15). The same throne that rewards the faithful shuts the door on those who loved falsehood and refused to repent. The verdict is real, and it goes two ways.

## 6. Freedom from Condemnation

So the great question of the courtroom becomes intensely personal. When my name is called and the books are opened, what will the verdict be?

And here is the gospel: *"Therefore, there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus, because through Christ Jesus the law of the Spirit who gives life has set you free from the law of sin and death"* (Romans 8:1–2). Read it again — *no condemnation.* Not less condemnation. Not condemnation we might survive. *No* condemnation, for those who are in Christ. The record of our deeds would condemn us; but the blood of Jesus has washed the robe, and our name is written in the book of life.

And notice who the Judge actually is: *"The Father judges no one, but has entrusted all judgment to the Son, that all may honor the Son just as they honor the Father"* (John 5:22–23). The One seated on the throne of judgment is Jesus Himself — the same Jesus who went to the cross to save us. The Judge of the courtroom of heaven is our Redeemer. For those who are in Him, the One holding the gavel is the One who already took our sentence.

## Challenge

This week, live as someone getting ready for a courtroom you cannot avoid — but do it without fear, because of Whose court it is.

First, examine your faith by the two marks Jesus named. Is it *persistent* — are you still asking, seeking, knocking on the thing you have been praying about, or did you quietly give up? And is it *active* — is there an obedience God has been asking of you that your belief has not yet obeyed? Pick up the widow's persistence again, and take the one step of obedience you have been delaying.

Then, settle the only question that ultimately matters: *is my name written in the book of life?* If you are in Christ, walk this week in the freedom of *no condemnation* — not carelessly, but gratefully, honoring the Son who took your verdict. If you are not yet sure, do not wait for the last day to find out. Come to the Judge now, while He is still the Savior.

**The court will be seated, and the books will be opened — but in Christ, there is now no condemnation.**
