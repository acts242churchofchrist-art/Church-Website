> *"…until we all reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God and become mature, attaining to the whole measure of the fullness of Christ."*
> — Ephesians 4:13

## Growing Is Not the Same as Maturing

Here is a question worth sitting with this morning: *how long have you been a Christian?*

Now here is the harder one: *has that time made you mature?*

Because those are two different questions. Time passes for everyone. Growth does not automatically follow, and maturity certainly does not. There are believers who have been in church for decades and are still living on milk, and there are younger believers whose character is already deeply settled in Christ.

So let us define what we are aiming at. **Spiritual maturity** is three things: a *lifelong, continuous process* of growing closer to God; *becoming more like Jesus Christ*; and *learning to live by the guidance of the Holy Spirit.*

And it is worth distinguishing it from growth. **Spiritual growth** is the active, ongoing process of learning, changing, and developing faith. **Spiritual maturity** is the advanced, stable state of character where those lessons have been deeply integrated into daily life and daily choices.

Growth is the learning. Maturity is when the learning has become *who you are.*

Paul put it in the simplest possible image: *"When I was a child, I talked like a child, I thought like a child, I reasoned like a child. When I became a man, I put the ways of childhood behind me"* (1 Corinthians 13:11).

Nobody scolds a child for talking like a child. That is exactly what a child should do. But there comes a point where the childish way of speaking, thinking, and reasoning must be put behind us — and Paul says it plainly: *when I became a man, I put it away.* That is our goal.

## Part 1 — Jesus Equips the Church to Mature

The first thing to understand is that God never expected us to mature on our own. He built the Church for exactly this purpose.

*"So Christ himself gave the apostles, the prophets, the evangelists, the pastors and teachers, to equip his people for works of service, so that the body of Christ may be built up until we all reach unity in the faith and in the knowledge of the Son of God and become mature, attaining to the whole measure of the fullness of Christ"* (Ephesians 4:11–13).

Look at the chain there. Christ gives leaders → to *equip* His people → for works of service → so the body is *built up* → until we all become **mature.** Every teacher, every preacher, every pastor exists for one destination: your maturity. The preaching is not entertainment and the teaching is not information. It is equipment.

And notice the measuring stick: *the whole measure of the fullness of Christ.* Spiritual maturity is not being better than the person next to you. It is being like Jesus. That is the standard, and it is why this is a lifelong pursuit.

So what does a mature believer actually look like? Paul gives us the sign: *"Then we will no longer be infants, tossed back and forth by the waves, and blown here and there by every wind of teaching and by the cunning and craftiness of people in their deceitful scheming. Instead, speaking the truth in love, we will grow to become in every respect the mature body of him who is the head, that is, Christ"* (Ephesians 4:14–15).

**The sign of maturity is stability.** An infant in the faith is *tossed* — by waves, by every new wind of teaching, by clever people with deceitful schemes. Every new idea shakes them. Every persuasive voice moves them. Every hardship knocks them over.

A mature believer is not moved that easily. Not because they are stubborn, but because they are *anchored* — they know the Son of God, and they know His Word well enough that a clever argument does not sweep them away.

And notice how the growing happens: *speaking the truth in love.* Not truth without love, which wounds. Not love without truth, which flatters. Both together — that is how the body matures.

## Part 2 — Maturity Is a Lifelong Process

The writer of Hebrews had to say something uncomfortable to a church that had stopped growing. Read it slowly, because it is meant to sting a little.

*"We have much to say about this, but it is hard to make it clear to you because you no longer try to understand. In fact, though by this time you ought to be teachers, you need someone to teach you the elementary truths of God's word all over again. You need milk, not solid food!"* (Hebrews 5:11–12).

*Though by this time you ought to be teachers.* That is the heartbreak of the verse. Enough time had passed that these believers should have been the ones teaching others. Instead they needed the basics explained again from the beginning.

And notice the reason given: *because you no longer try to understand.* The problem was not capacity. It was effort. They had stopped pressing in.

That is a real danger for any of us. You can attend for years, hear hundreds of sermons, and still be spiritually an infant — if you never move from hearing to applying, from milk to solid food. **Immaturity is to our own disadvantage.**

Then comes the definition of maturity that we should all write down: *"Anyone who lives on milk, being still an infant, is not acquainted with the teaching about righteousness. But solid food is for the mature, who by constant use have trained themselves to distinguish good from evil"* (Hebrews 5:13–14).

**The sign of maturity is the trained ability to distinguish good from evil.**

Look at the two words: *constant use* and *trained.* This is athletic language. Nobody becomes discerning by accident or overnight. It comes from repeatedly using what you have learned — choosing right, rejecting wrong, applying the Word in real decisions — until discernment becomes reflexive.

That is why a mature believer can sense when something is off before they can fully explain why. They have used the Word so constantly that their spiritual senses have been trained. And it is why an immature believer, however sincere, is so easily deceived by things that merely *look* good.

## Part 3 — The Parable of the Barren Fig Tree

Now Jesus tells a story that gives this whole subject weight and urgency.

*"Then he told this parable: 'A man had a fig tree growing in his vineyard, and he went to look for fruit on it but did not find any. So he said to the man who took care of the vineyard, "For three years now I've been coming to look for fruit on this fig tree and haven't found any. Cut it down! Why should it use up the soil?"'"* (Luke 13:6–7).

Three things stand out.

**The owner came *looking* for fruit.** He did not admire the leaves or measure the height. He came expecting fruit. And God expects spiritual fruit from us. A tree planted in good soil, tended and watered, is supposed to produce something.

**Three years is not a short time.** This was patience, not impatience. The tree had been given every reasonable opportunity.

**And the charge is striking:** *"Why should it use up the soil?"* A fruitless tree is not neutral — it is consuming resources that could go to something fruitful. That is a sobering thought for any believer who has been receiving for years without producing.

But then comes the most beautiful line in the parable: *"'Sir,' the man replied, 'leave it alone for one more year, and I'll dig around it and fertilize it. If it bears fruit next year, fine! If not, then cut it down'"* (Luke 13:8–9).

**Jesus intercedes for us.** That is the keeper of the vineyard. When judgment was justified, the keeper asked for more time — and not just more time, but more *care*: *I'll dig around it and fertilize it.* He does not merely delay the sentence; he goes to work on the tree.

That is our Lord. When we have been unfruitful, He intercedes for another year, and He tends us — through the Word, through correction, through hard seasons that break up the soil around our roots.

But hear the other half honestly: **grace is not endless.** *"If not, then cut it down."* The extension was real, but so was the deadline. The mercy of God is not permission to stay unfruitful forever. It is an opportunity, and it should be received with urgency.

## Part 4 — Five Keys to Spiritual Maturity

So how do we actually mature? Not by wishing, and not by waiting. Here are five keys.

### 1. Studying the Bible

Read and study Scripture *every day.* Use God's Word to guide your choices — not just to feel encouraged, but to actually decide things. And meditate on biblical truths, so that you gain wisdom and have something ready when temptation comes.

This is how you move from milk to solid food. The believer who only hears the Word on Sunday is being fed once a week; the mature believer is eating daily.

### 2. Prayer

Talk *and listen* to God regularly. Share your needs, your fears, and your thanksgiving with Him. And build a steady habit of quiet time so you stay close to Him.

Notice the word *habit.* Maturity is rarely built in dramatic moments; it is built in the unglamorous consistency of showing up to talk with God when nothing in particular is happening.

### 3. Walking in the Holy Spirit

Let the Holy Spirit lead your daily life step by step. Show the fruit of the Spirit — love, joy, peace, self-control. Turn away from old habits and let your heart genuinely change.

Notice again that fruit is the evidence. Not knowledge alone, not attendance alone — fruit. And notice that walking in the Spirit is *step by step*, not one giant leap.

### 4. Showing Humility

Let go of pride and admit when you fall short. Stay open and willing to receive correction — from God *and from others.* Be quick to repent and quick to ask forgiveness.

This one separates the mature from the merely knowledgeable. A person who cannot receive correction has capped their own growth, no matter how much Scripture they know. Maturity is measured partly by how quickly you can say *I was wrong.*

### 5. Serving Others

Help the people around you with a pure and kind heart. Give your time and your love without expecting any reward. Look out for the needs of others before your own desires.

This is the fruit that proves the rest is real. A servant heart like Jesus is the clearest evidence that a person has actually matured, because self-centeredness is the last thing to die in any of us.

## Nobody Has Arrived

If all of that feels like a lot, hear Paul's honesty. This is the apostle who wrote most of the New Testament:

*"Not that I have already obtained all this, or have already arrived at my goal, but I press on to take hold of that for which Christ Jesus took hold of me. Brothers and sisters, I do not consider myself yet to have taken hold of it. But one thing I do: Forgetting what is behind and straining toward what is ahead, I press on toward the goal to win the prize for which God has called me heavenward in Christ Jesus"* (Philippians 3:12–14).

*Not that I have already arrived.* Paul. If Paul had not arrived, none of us has. Maturity is not a finish line we cross and then relax; it is a direction we keep walking.

And look at the two movements he describes: *forgetting what is behind* and *straining toward what is ahead.* Some of us cannot mature because we will not let go of what is behind — old failures we keep rehearsing, old victories we keep resting on, old wounds we keep protecting. Paul lets go of all of it and strains forward.

**Christ took hold of you first.** That is the beautiful part. You are pressing on to take hold of the very thing for which He already took hold of you. He is not waiting to see if you qualify — He has already claimed you, and now He invites you to grow into it.

## Challenge

This week, pick the key you have been avoiding.

Read the five again honestly and find the one you have quietly skipped. For some it is the *daily* in Bible study — you read when convenient, not consistently. For others it is *listening* in prayer, because you only talk. For others it is **humility** — there is a correction you received months ago that you still have not accepted. For others it is **serving**, because you have been receiving for years without giving.

Pick that one, and practice it every day this week. Not all five. One.

Then ask the fig tree question about your own life: *what fruit would someone find if they came looking?* Not leaves — not attendance, not knowledge, not how long you have been here. **Fruit.** And if the honest answer is *not much*, take heart: the Keeper of the vineyard is already interceding for you, already digging around your roots, already at work. But do not waste the extra year.

*Forget what is behind. Strain toward what is ahead. Press on.*

**He has already taken hold of you — now grow into the fullness of Christ.**
